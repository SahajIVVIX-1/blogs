BoW vectors are huge, sparse, and know nothing about meaning ("car" and "automobile" share no dimension). **word2vec** (Mikolov et al., 2013) learns a **dense** vector (say 100 numbers) per word:

- **Skip-gram**: predict the context words from the centre word.
- **CBOW**: predict the centre word from its context.
- **Negative sampling** turns this into cheap binary classification tasks.

This is the distributional hypothesis: words that appear in similar contexts get similar vectors. I trained skip-gram with gensim on scikit-learn's 20 Newsgroups posts (about 11k training documents). That's tiny by word2vec standards, so the neighbours are noisy but recognisable.

```python run
import re
from gensim.models import Word2Vec
from sklearn.datasets import fetch_20newsgroups

posts = fetch_20newsgroups(subset="train", remove=("headers", "footers", "quotes")).data
sentences = [re.findall(r"[a-z]+", p.lower()) for p in posts]
sentences = [s for s in sentences if len(s) > 3]
print("documents:", len(sentences), "| tokens:", sum(map(len, sentences)))

model = Word2Vec(sentences, vector_size=100, window=5, min_count=5, sg=1, workers=1, seed=0, epochs=5)
print("vocabulary:", len(model.wv), "| vector shape:", model.wv["computer"].shape)
for w in ["windows", "god", "car", "space"]:
    print(f"{w:>8} ->", [n for n, _ in model.wv.most_similar(w, topn=5)])
print("sim(car, engine) =", round(float(model.wv.similarity("car", "engine")), 3),
      "| sim(car, god) =", round(float(model.wv.similarity("car", "god")), 3))
```
